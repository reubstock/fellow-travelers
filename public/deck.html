02 02 / 10
The Problem
Men are more isolated — and better at hiding it — than ever.
Despite accelerating technical progress, life feels harder to navigate. Not materially — existentially. The networks men have built optimize for utility, not honesty.
  • Male loneliness is at a historic high across developed countries.
  • Professional networks reward performance over candor.
  • There is no modern container for men to think together without an agenda.
  • The animating question: why, despite all this progress, does something feel missing?
03 03 / 10
The Insight
Something changes when men start telling the truth.
The dyad — two men being genuinely honest with each other — produces a kind of trust that is rare and hard to manufacture. Scale that up carefully, in a private setting, and something real happens.
The Dyad

One-on-one honesty between members creates the foundation. We borrow from AA's sponsor model — without the seniority.

The Group

Small, consistent, private. The same men, the same place, month after month. Consistency is the product.

04 04 / 10
What It Is
Private homes. Monthly dinners. No agenda.
Fellows meet in each other's homes — not clubs, not conference rooms. The setting is intentional. So is everything it rules out.
  • 90–120 minutes. A short opening, open discussion, one question to leave with.
  • Modified Chatham House rules — ideas can travel, identities don't.
  • No pitching. No networking. No coaching. No devices.
  • Leadership rotates. There is no permanent leader at any level.

07 07 / 10
The Rules
Simple. Sharp. Designed to protect something real.
Rules taken seriously tend to create cultures worth having. We drew on AA, Chatham House, Milk & Honey, and Ben Franklin's Junto — then simplified.
01

Confidentiality

Ideas can leave the room. Identities cannot. No attribution without explicit permission.

02

No instrumentality

We are not here to get anything from each other. Friendships and projects that emerge stay outside the group container.

03

Presence

No phones, no multitasking, no slipping out. You are here or you are not.

04

Holding to account

We gently point out when principles are being violated — in ourselves as much as in each other.

05

No PR

No posts about the group. No using membership to signal virtue or exclusivity. Attraction, not promotion.

08 08 / 10
The Network
Groups multiply. Standards hold.
Each group is autonomous. No franchise, no headquarters, no hierarchy. The network grows through quality, not promotion — the same way AA does.
New groupsNeed a founding member and a starter kit. New members voted in by the existing group.
VisitingAny member of one group can attend any other. The network is horizontal.
The dyadStructured one-on-one relationships between members — the sponsor model, without seniority.
Decision-makingSmallest group affected decides. One person proposes; each man responds. No Roberts Rules.
ScaleMany groups. Consistent culture. Difficult to fake.

Inspirations
We've stood on good shoulders.
Every element of this has been done before, in pieces. We're combining them.
Ben Franklin's Junto (1727)Mutual improvement. Rotating topics. No seniority. Still the best model for peer learning.
Alcoholics AnonymousConsistency of time and place. The sponsor dyad. Leaderless structure. No dues. It works.
F3Culture built through a shared lexicon. Peer accountability without a commercial layer.
Burning ManSelf-organizing. Non-commercial. Rules that protect the container rather than monetize it.
The Twilight ClubMen of different fields. No agenda beyond honest conversation. A model from 1883 that still makes sense.
